\section{Internal Incompressible Viscous Flow}

\subsection{Internal Flow Characteristics}

\subsubsection{Laminar Versus Turbulent Flow}
The pipe flow regime, i.e. if the flow is turbulent or laminar, is determined by the Reynolds number, briefly mentioned in \Cref{sec:sigdim}, $\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho \overline{V} D}{\mu}$.
Under normal conditions, the transition to turbulence occurs at roughly $\mathrm{Re} \approx \num{2300} $ for flow in pipes.
For water in a $\textit{Ø}=\qty{1}{in.}$ pipe, this corresponds to an average speed of $V \approx \qty{0,1}{\frac{m}{s}}$.
Turbulence occurs when the viscous forces are not able to damp out the random fluctuations in fluid motion.
This means that a fluid of higher viscosity (e.g. motor oil compared to water) is able to be flowing at a higher rate before becoming turbulent.
On the other hand, high-density fluids will exacerbate the inertial forces due to random fluctuations in motion, and therefore this fluid will become turbulent at a lower flow rate.


\subsubsection{The Entrance Region}
\begin{figure} [ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{./figures/f8_1.png}
  \caption{Flow in the entrance region of a pipe.}
  \label{fig:f8_1}
\end{figure}

On \Cref{fig:f8_1} laminar flow in the entrance region of a circular pipe is portrayed.
The flow has uniform velocity $U_0$ at the pipe entrance.
Due to the no-slip condition, we know that the velocity along the wall must be zero along the entire length of the pipe.
Therefore, a boundary layer will develop along the wall.
As the fluid flows through the pipe the shear force from the pipe walls will slowly lead to this boundary layer expanding until the fully developed velocity profile is reached.
For incompressible flow, mass conservation requires that as the speed close to the wall is reduced, the speed in the central frictionless region of the pipe must increase to compensate.

A \textit{fully developed flow} is one which does not change velocity profile with distance.
The distance from the entrance to the point at which the flow is fully developed is termed the \textit{entrance length}.

For laminar flow the entrance length $L$, is a function of Reynolds number,
\[ 
\frac{L}{D} \approx \num{0,06} \frac{\rho \overline{V} D}{\mu}
\]
where $\overline{V} = \frac{Q}{A}$ is the average velocity.
If the flow is instead turbulent, the enhanced mixing of fluid layers will lead to the boundary layer growing quicker and therefore lead to a shorter entrance length.


\subsection{Fully Developed Laminar Flow Between Infinite Parallel Plates}

\subsubsection{Both Plates Stationary}
Fluid in high-pressure hydraulic systems, such as automobile brake systems, often leaks through the annular gap between a piston and cylinder, as shown on \Cref{fig:f8_2}.
For very small gaps this flow field may be modeled as flow between infinite parallel plates.
To calculate the leakage flow rate, we will first determine the velocity field.

\begin{figure} [ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{./figures/f8_2.png}
  \caption{Piston-cylinder system approximated as infinite parallel plates.}
  \label{fig:f8_2}
\end{figure}

We consider the fully developed laminar flow between horizontal infinite plates.
The plates are separated by a distance $a$ and considered infinite in the $z$-direction with no variation of any fluid property in this direction.
The flow is also assumes steady and incompressible.
From the no-slip condition the boundary conditions are
\begin{align*}
  y &= 0 &\implies u &=0 \\
  y &= a &\implies u &=0
.\end{align*}

Since the flow is fully developed it cannot vary with $x$ and, hence, depends only on $y$, so that $u = u(y)$.
Furthermore, there will be no component of velocity in the $y$ or $z$ directions, as the flow is fully developed.

\begin{figure} [ht]
  \begin{subfigure}{0.48\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{./figures/f8_3a.png}
    \caption{Geometry of CV}
  \end{subfigure}
  \begin{subfigure}{0.48\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{./figures/f8_3b.png}
    \caption{Forces acting on CV}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Control volume for analysis of laminar flow between stationary infinite parallel plates.}
  \label{fig:f8_3}
\end{figure}

We consider a differential control volume of side $\mathrm{d}V = \mathrm{d}x \, \mathrm{d}y \, \mathrm{d}z$ as shown on \cref{fig:f8_3}.
To this control volume we apply the momentum equation in the $x$ direction.
\[ 
F_{S_x} + F_{B_x} = \frac{\partial }{\partial t} \int_{\mathrm{CV}} u \rho \, \mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{CS}} u \rho \Vec{V} \cdot \mathrm{d} \Vec{A}
.\]
In this $F_{B_x} = 0$ due to the assumed geometry and as the flow is assumed steady the first term on the RHS will also equal zero.
Hence,
\[ 
F_{S_x} = \int_{\mathrm{CS}} u \rho \Vec{V} \cdot \mathrm{d} \Vec{A}
.\]

For fully developed flow, a basic feature is that the velocity profile is the same at all locations along the flow; hence there is no change in momentum. Therefore the above equation reduces to the simple result,
\[ 
F_{S_x} = 0
.\]

Therefore the next step is to sum up the forces acting on the control volume in the $x$ direction.
These are the pressure forces on the left and right faces and the shear forces on the top and bottom faces.

If we assume the pressure at the center of the element to be $p$, then the pressure force on the left face is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_{L} = \left( p - \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2} \right) \, \mathrm{d}y \, \mathrm{d}z
\]
and the pressure force on the right face is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_R = - \left( p + \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2} \right)\, \mathrm{d}y \, \mathrm{d}z
.\]

Likewise, if the shear stress at the center of the element is assumed to be $\tau_{yx}$, then the shear force on the bottom face is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_B = - \left( \tau_{yx} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{yx}}{\mathrm{d}y} \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{2} \right) \, \mathrm{d}x \, \mathrm{d}z
\]
and the shear force on the top face is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_T = \left( \tau_{yx} + \frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{yx}}{\mathrm{d}y} \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{2} \right) \, \mathrm{d}x \, \mathrm{d}z
.\]
Inserting all of these forces in to the momentum equation from before yields
\[ 
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{yx}}{\mathrm{d}y} 
.\]
This equation states that as the momentum is constant, the pressure force balances the friction force.
Here the LHS is only a function of $x$ and the $RHS$ is only a function of $y$.
Hence, the only way for it to be valid for all $x$ and $y$ is if each side is in fact constant:
\[ 
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} =\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{yx}}{\mathrm{d}y} = \mathrm{constant}
.\]
Upon integration of this we obtain
\[ 
\tau_{yx} = \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)y + c_1
\]
which indicates that the shear stress varies linearly with $y$. 
For a Newtonian fluid in one dimension the shear stress is also given by
\[ 
\tau_{yx} = \mu \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}y} 
\]
Upon equating these and integrating for $y$ the following expression is reached
\[ 
u = \frac{1}{2\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)y^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)ay = \frac{a^2}{2\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \left( \left( \frac{y}{a} \right)^2 - \left( \frac{y}{a} \right) \right)
.\]
This is the velocity profile of the flow.

The shear stress distribution is then
\[ 
\tau_{yx} = \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)y + c_1 = \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)y - \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)a = a \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \left( \frac{y}{a} - \frac{1}{2} \right)
.\]

The volume flow rate per unit depth is
\[ 
\frac{Q}{l} = - \frac{1}{12\mu} \left( \frac{- \Delta p}{L} \right)a^3 = \frac{a^3 \Delta p}{12 \mu L}
.\]

The average velocity $\overline{V}$ is given by
\[ 
\overline{V} = \frac{Q}{A} = - \frac{1}{12\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \frac{a^3 l }{la} = - \frac{1}{12\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)a^2
.\]

The point of maximum velocity will be at $y = \frac{a}{2}$ where the velocity will be
\[ 
u_{y = \frac{a}{2}} = u_{\mathrm{max}} = - \frac{1}{8\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)a^2 = \frac{3}{2} \overline{V}
.\]


\subsubsection{Upper Plate Moving with Constant Speed}
The second basic way to generate flow between infinite parallel plates, is to have one plate moving parallel to the either.

In this case the boundary conditions are
\begin{align*}
  u &= 0 & &\implies & y &= 0 \\
  u &= U & &\implies & y &=a
.\end{align*}

In this case the velocity distribution is also given by
\[ 
u = \frac{1}{2\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)y^2 + \frac{c_1}{\mu}y + c_2
.\]
Evaluating for $c_1$ and $c_2$ using the boundary conditions yields
\[ 
u = \frac{Uy}{a} + \frac{a^2}{2\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \left(  \left( \frac{y}{a} \right)^2 - \left( \frac{y}{a} \right) \right)
.\]

The shear stress distribution will be
\[ 
\tau_{yx} = \mu \frac{U}{a} + \frac{a^2}{2} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \left( \frac{2y}{a^2} - \frac{1}{a} \right) = \mu \frac{U}{a} + a \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \left( \frac{y}{a} - \frac{1}{2} \right)
.\]

The volume flow rate per unit depth is
\[ 
\frac{Q}{l} = \frac{Ua}{2} - \frac{1}{12\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) a^3
.\]

The average velocity magnitude $\overline{V}$ is
\[ 
\overline{V} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{l \left( \frac{Ua}{2} - \frac{1}{12\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) a^3 \right)}{la} = \frac{U}{2} - \frac{1}{12\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) a^2
.\]

The point of maximum velocity will be at
\[ 
y = \frac{a}{2} - \frac{\frac{U}{a}}{\left( \frac{1}{\mu} \right) \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)}
.\]
However there is no simple relationship for relating the max flow velocity at this point to the mean velocity in this case.


\subsection{Fully Developed Laminar Flow in a Pipe}
Flow in a pipe is axisymmetric.
Consequently it is most convenient to work in cylindrical coordinates when describing these.

For a fully developed steady flow, the $x$ component of the momentum equation has been shown above to reduce to
\[ 
F_{S_x} = 0
.\]

\begin{figure} [ht]
  \begin{subfigure}{0.24\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./figures/f8_7a.png}
    \caption{End view of CV}
  \end{subfigure}
  \begin{subfigure}{0.34\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./figures/f8_7b.png}
    \caption{Side view CV}
  \end{subfigure}
  \begin{subfigure}{0.39\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./figures/f8_7c.png}
    \caption{Forces on CV}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Differential control volume for analysis of fully developed laminar flow in a pipe.}
  \label{fig:f8_7}
\end{figure}

We consider the differential control volume shown on \Cref{fig:f8_7}.
Using the same notion as in the previous section the pressure force on the left face of the control volume is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_L = p 2\pi r \, \mathrm{d}r
\]
and the pressure force on the right end is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_R = - \left( p + \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} \, \mathrm{d}x \right) 2\pi r \, \mathrm{d}r
.\]

The shear force on the inner cylindrical surface of the control volume is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_I = - \tau_{rx} 2\pi r \, \mathrm{d}x
\]
and the shear force on the outer cylindrical surface is
\[ 
\mathrm{d}F_O = \left( \tau_{rx} + \frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{rx}}{\mathrm{d}r} \, \mathrm{d}r  \right) 2 \pi \left( r + \mathrm{d}r \right)\, \mathrm{d}x
.\]

Substituting all of these into the momentum equation from before leads to
\[ 
- \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} 2\pi r \, \mathrm{d}r \, \mathrm{d}x + \tau_{rx} 2\pi \, \mathrm{d}r \, \mathrm{d}x  + \frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{rx}}{\mathrm{d}r} 2\pi r \, \mathrm{d}r \, \mathrm{d}x = 0
.\]
Dividing this by $2\pi r$ and solving for $\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} $ gives
\[ 
  \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} = \frac{\tau_{rx}}{r} + \frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{rx}}{\mathrm{d}r} = \frac{1}{r} \frac{\mathrm{d} \left( r \tau_{rx} \right)}{\mathrm{d}r} 
.\]
Here the same argument as we used for the parallel plates of the LHS only depending on $x$ and the RHS only depending on $r$ meaning both must be constant:
\[ 
\frac{1}{r} \frac{\mathrm{d} \left( r \tau_{rx} \right)}{\mathrm{d}r} = \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} = \mathrm{constant} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\left( r \tau_{rx} \right)}{\mathrm{d}r}  = r \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x} 
.\]
This shows that in a constant diameter pipe, the pressure drops uniformly along the pipe length. Integrating this and employing the boundary conditions yields
\[ 
u = - \frac{R^2}{4\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) \left( 1 - \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2 \right)
.\]

The shear stress distribution is
\[ 
  \tau_{rx} = \mu \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}r}  = \frac{r}{2} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)
.\]

The volume flow rate is
\[ 
Q = -\frac{\pi R^{4}}{8\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) = \frac{\pi \Delta p R^{4}}{8 \mu L}
.\]

The average velocity is
\[ 
\overline{V} = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{Q}{\pi R^2} = - \frac{R^2}{8 \mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right)
.\]

The point of maximum velocity will be directly at the center of the pipe where the velocity will be
\[ 
u_{r = 0} = u_{\mathrm{max}} = - \frac{R^2}{4\mu} \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}  \right) = 2 \overline{V}
.\]
